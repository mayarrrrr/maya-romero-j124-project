## A Closer Look at Billboard Hot 100 Number Ones

This dataset originates from Chris Dalla Riva, a musical artist and Senior Product Manager at Audiomack who specializes in data analytics, as he was writing his book Uncharted Territory: What Numbers Tell Us about the Biggest Hit Songs and Ourselves. Dalla Riva also uses this dataset for his own newsletter, Can’t Get Much Higher. This dataset lists every Number One song on the Billboard Hot 100 Chart from August 4, 1958 to January 11, 2025, as well as a wide range of additional information about the song. Since this dataset specifically comes from Billboard’s own charting and the creator was using this information for a book to discuss various music analytics, I would believe this to be trustworthy and unbiased.  

I created four different Pivot tables based off of this dataset. We can look at different inputs/values based on given information such as the race of who produced the song or if the song involves any samples. In this first Pivot table, I’m looking at how many weeks a song was at number one and how many were written for a TV show. I thought it was interesting to see that there were 16 songs in total, and none charted after week 4. In the second Pivot, I’m looking again at how many weeks a song was at number one, but this time in connection to a song’s danceability. I was surprised to see that 26,632 songs were only in week 1 compared to only 160 songs in week 19. For the last Pivot, this one was pretty interesting as I’m looking at different sound effects and how many artists used them. These range from an airplane to a duck to a typewriter. The highest number of sound effect used was a crowd at 7 artists.

While music data is a fun topic to cover, it also tells us a lot about how people react to music. For example, is the song a response to something happening in the world? How does its genre play an impact in how people react to it, and how can location affect genre listening habits? How popular is the artist? Does artist gender play a role in how many weeks a song charts at number one? How do listening habits change by country? These are all questions that may come into play when analyzing a dataset such as this one. I believe that music is one of the most impactful forms of media and art in our world, and in return can speak volumes about the human experience in regards to what kinds of music people are listening to the most. I thought that this dataset in particular would be interesting to look at because music charting is something that’s been happening for decades, and although I was primarily focused on the past year or so, it just go to show how vast this data really is and may give more insight into how people’s listening habits have potentially changed over the years.
